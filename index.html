Understanding the opportunity

Your capital is attached to a physical asset.

A non-operated working interest is not a paper position. It is a direct interest in a specific well, drilled by a specific operator, on a specific pad, on a schedule set by permits, rigs, and crews rather than by markets.

That is the reason the tax treatment exists at all, and it is also the reason the risk is real. Everything in the session follows from this one fact.

01Real operations. Permitting, drilling, completion and production each take time and capital, and each carries its own way of going wrong.
02Real operators. Who drills the well is the largest single variable in the outcome — larger than the geology in most cases.
03Real risk. Wells can underperform or fail to produce commercially. Capital committed is capital at risk of total loss.

Tax considerations

Why this belongs in a conversation with your CPA.

Direct working interests are treated differently from most investments because the costs of drilling a well behave differently from buying an asset. Where that treatment applies, what it attaches to, and whether it reaches a given investor at all depends on the structure, the timing, and that investor’s own position.

01Cost categories differ. Intangible costs, tangible equipment and depletion are three separate mechanisms with three separate sets of conditions.
02Timing is substantive. When capital is deployed relative to the drilling calendar and the tax year is frequently the whole question, not a detail.
03Circumstances govern. Two investors in the same well can face different outcomes depending on income type and what their return already contains.
04It sits beside your CPA. The session is designed to give you better questions to bring them, not to replace their advice.

No deduction, tax treatment, or outcome is promised or implied. Nothing here is tax advice. Consult your own CPA, tax adviser and legal counsel before making any decision.

How oil & gas wells work

One well, from surface to lateral.

A working interest follows the physical work. Understanding the sequence below is what lets you judge where costs arise, when timing matters, and what is actually at risk at each stage.

Surface operations

The rig, power units, tanks, pipe racks and crew facilities. This is where drilling is powered and controlled, and where most of the visible capital sits during the drilling phase.

Vertical wellbore

The bore is drilled straight down through shallow formations. Successive casing strings are run and cemented in place to isolate groundwater and stabilise the hole before drilling continues.

Build section

The curve. Over a measured radius the bore turns from vertical to horizontal, steered to arrive inside the target interval at the correct depth and direction.

Horizontal lateral

The bore runs within the target formation, often for thousands of feet. Exposing far more reservoir rock than a vertical well is the entire reason for drilling this way. It is later perforated and stimulated so hydrocarbons can flow in.

Geological formations

Layered sedimentary rock laid down over geological time. The darker interval the lateral follows is the organic-rich target; the layers above it act as seals that keep hydrocarbons contained.

Due diligence & risk considerations

The operator is the largest variable.

Who drills the well matters more than almost anything else in the outcome. A capable operator on an ordinary prospect generally beats a weak operator on a strong one, which is why diligence starts with people and track record rather than with projections.

The session covers what to ask, what good answers sound like, and which answers should end the conversation.

—Track record. What has this operator drilled, where, and what actually happened to those wells?
—Alignment. Does the sponsor invest their own capital on the same terms as yours?
—Obligations. What is owed beyond the initial contribution, and under what circumstances?
—Downside. What happens to your capital if the well underperforms? Total loss is a real outcome.

Scale and context

A field is not a single well.

Programmes are usually drilled across multiple wells and multiple pads. How capital is spread across them is a question worth asking any sponsor before committing to one.

Questions

Before you register.

Who is this session for?

Accredited investors and high-income professionals who consistently pay substantial federal tax and want to understand how oil and gas tax structures work before deciding whether they are relevant. It is educational, not an offer.

Does attending commit me to anything?

No. Nothing is offered or sold on the session, and there is no obligation of any kind. Attendees who want to go further can request a suitability conversation afterwards; most will not, and that is a legitimate outcome.

Are oil and gas investments suitable for everyone?

No. All investing carries risk, and these are private, illiquid, long-horizon investments that are unsuitable for many people regardless of income. Potential tax treatment depends on the specific structure and on your individual circumstances. The session covers these limits directly.

Will specific returns or tax savings be presented?

No. No projections, guaranteed savings, or performance figures are presented. The session explains how structures are designed to work and what determines whether a benefit applies at all.

Is it live or recorded?

Each session includes live Q&A. Registrants who cannot attend receive access to a replay.

Why has my CPA never raised this?

Most CPAs are engaged to report accurately on what already happened rather than to explore alternative structures ahead of time. Oil and gas sits where tax law, energy markets, and private investing overlap, which falls outside many practices’ scope.

What happens after the session?

You receive the replay and a short summary. If you want to explore whether the strategy applies to your situation, you can request a one-to-one suitability call. There is no follow-up pressure either way.
