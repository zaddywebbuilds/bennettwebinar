01

Section one

Why this asset class draws high-income capital.

Four different things people call “oil and gas”

The structure is what determines treatment.

Energy equities

Public stock

You own shares in a company. Liquid. No direct tax attributes pass to you.

Royalties

Revenue interest

A share of production revenue, free of operating costs. Different treatment again.

Funds

Pooled vehicle

A manager allocates across projects. Attributes depend entirely on the fund’s own structure.

What we discuss

Non-operated working interest

A direct interest in the well itself. You are a capital partner, not an operator.

The word is the same in all four cases. The tax treatment is not. Almost every misunderstanding starts here.

What non-operated means

A capital partner, not an operator.

·

The operator runs the well. They permit it, drill it, complete it, and manage production. Some are large public names, others are private teams most investors never encounter.

·

You contribute capital for a share of the interest. No property to manage, no staff, no daily operational decisions.

·

You hold a direct working interest. That direct ownership is precisely why the tax attributes of the drilling activity can reach your return, rather than stopping at a corporate entity.

·

It also means you carry the risk directly. We will spend real time on that in section four.

02

Section two

Where the deductions actually come from.

Three distinct mechanisms

Not one deduction. Three, with different rules.

Mechanism one

Intangible drilling costs

The non-salvageable costs of drilling: labour, fuel, fluids, site prep. The portion of a well that leaves nothing behind to sell.

Mechanism two

Tangible costs

Equipment with salvage value: casing, wellhead, pumping units. Treated differently from intangibles and recovered over time.

Mechanism three

Depletion

An allowance recognising that a reservoir is a finite resource being consumed as it produces.

The part that gets skipped

Every one of these carries conditions: on the structure, on your participation, on timing, and on your own tax position. A headline percentage quoted without those conditions is not information. It is marketing.

Mechanism one, in plain terms

Intangible costs are the spend that leaves nothing to sell.

Drilling a well consumes labour, fuel, drilling fluid, and site preparation. When the work is done, none of that can be recovered and resold. Tax policy has long treated that category differently from equipment, because encouraging domestic drilling was a deliberate choice by Congress, not an accident or a loophole.

Conditions, not footnotes

Whether any of this reaches your return depends on how the interest is held, the nature of your participation, when the capital was deployed, and your own circumstances. Two investors in the same well can face different outcomes.

Why the headline numbers mislead

The same well, two different investors.

Determines outcome

How the interest is held

Direct working interest, versus a pooled or corporate wrapper.

Determines outcome

When capital was deployed

Relative to the drilling calendar and the tax year.

Determines outcome

Your own tax position

Income type, offsetting activity, and what your return already contains.

This is why I will not give you a number tonight. Anyone who gives you one before knowing these three things is guessing, and you should treat it accordingly.

03

Section three

Why timing decides the outcome.

The calendar is a mechanism, not a sales tactic

The same decision, eight weeks apart.

·

Drilling has a schedule. A well is permitted, spudded, drilled, and completed on a timeline that does not bend to anyone’s tax year.

·

Tax years close. Whether activity falls inside one year or the next decides the outcome. For this asset class it is frequently the whole question.

·

Rushing is its own risk. A December decision made without diligence is a bad decision that happens to be timely. That is not an improvement.

The deadline is real. Start the evaluation early enough that you never have to hurry the decision.

04

Section four

The risks, stated directly.

What can go wrong

This is where capital is actually lost.

01

Geological risk. Wells can underperform or fail to produce commercially. Capital committed to a well that does not deliver is capital at risk of total loss.

02

Commodity price exposure. Returns depend on prices you do not control and nobody reliably predicts.

03

Illiquidity. There is no exit button. Assume your capital is committed for the life of the project.

04

Operator quality. The single largest variable. A good operator on a mediocre prospect beats a poor operator on a strong one.

05

Capital calls and cost overruns. Understand your obligations beyond the initial contribution before you commit anything.

The distinction that matters most

A potential benefit is not a guaranteed one.

What people hear

“A guaranteed write-off”

A fixed outcome, certain in advance, independent of circumstances.

What is true

A conditional treatment

A tax treatment that may apply, depending on structure, timing, participation, and your individual position, applied to an investment that can lose money.

Worth saying plainly

A tax benefit on an investment that loses capital is not a win. The investment has to make sense on its own merits first. If it only works because of the tax treatment, it does not work.

05

Section five

The questions to ask before you write a check.

Take this to any sponsor

Eight questions. Including to me.

01

Who is the operator, what have they drilled before, and what happened?

02

Exactly what interest am I acquiring, and how is it held?

03

What is the full cost structure, including every fee and who receives it?

04

What obligations exist beyond my initial contribution?

05

What is the realistic timeline from commitment to first production?

06

What happens to my capital if this well underperforms?

07

Does the sponsor invest their own money on the same terms as mine?

08

What documentation will I receive, and when?

How to raise it with your CPA

Change the question you bring them.

Backward looking

“What do I owe?”

Asked in March, about a year that has already closed. Nothing can be changed by then.

Forward looking

“What should I be evaluating before year end, and what would rule it out?”

Asked with time to act, and specific enough to get a real answer.

This is designed to sit beside your CPA, never to replace them. Bring them the structure and let them tell you how it interacts with your actual return.
